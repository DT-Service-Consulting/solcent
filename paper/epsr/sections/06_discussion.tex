\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Post-processing and the validation reference}
\label{sec:disc-mos}

The MOS correction works at the level it was built for, and the gain then disappears in the PV model: the systematic component is a small part of the irradiance error, and a learner trained on the biased input has already fitted around it. For a physical model chain, which propagates irradiance deterministically, better irradiance does give better power \citep{Mayer2021,Mayer2022}; for a statistical model trained on the same distribution the gain may not survive, and only an end-to-end test shows whether it does.

The reference matters as much. Scored against ERA5, which shares its model and resolution, the forecast looks 1.39 times better than against an independent satellite record that tracks measured PV more closely. A quarter of that gap is resolution; the rest is agreement between two products of the same model. Agreement with a reanalysis from the same centre is not evidence of forecast quality.

\subsection{Transfer: hyperparameters and site information}
\label{sec:disc-transfer}

Hyperparameters selected on a chronological split reward memorising individual rooftops, because those rooftops recur in the test set; on unseen rooftops this becomes a liability. The finding should apply to any fleet model tuned where all units are seen and deployed where they are not, and the remedy, a grouped split in the inner loop of the search, is cheap.

The results agree with SolNet \citep{Depoortere2025} that a fleet-trained model serves a new site from its first day, but differ in what local data is for: there a locally trained model needs about half a year to beat transfer, here a two-parameter rescaling of the transferred forecast helps after about a week (Section~\ref{sec:res-deploy}). SolNet's hourly, min--max-scaled errors are not directly comparable with ours (Supplementary material).

The ablation shows what an unseen rooftop needs. Reported capacity, tilt and azimuth help a seen rooftop mainly by identifying it; two days of its own output, through the lagged performance ratio, carry its effective capacity and prevent most failures. Which limit binds therefore depends on the setting. For seen rooftops it is the weather input (Section~\ref{sec:res-limit}); for unseen ones it is also the installation record, and excluding a single defective rooftop moves the pooled $R^2$ by 0.0115, against 0.0046 for the best weather-input change. Tilt and azimuth can be inferred from generation data \citep{Meng2020}, and effective capacity estimated from a week or more of output (Section~\ref{sec:res-deploy}). \ifepsr Until then, a grid operator or aggregator should screen new installations for implausible metadata before relying on their forecasts.% [EPSR-SCOPE]

\subsection{What would move the limit}
\label{sec:disc-limit}

The oracles show where the value lies. Knowing the fleet-wide sky is worth little; knowing the sky within a few kilometres is worth +0.065 in $R^2$, and forecast errors decorrelate over tens of kilometres. The constraint is the placement and timing of cloud at kilometre scales, not the statistical layer. Higher-resolution NWP is the direct route, untested here: the NWP source affects PV accuracy materially \citep{Mayer2023}, and convection-permitting models resolve cloud structure that a 0.25$^\circ$ grid cannot, although whether that skill survives to a day-ahead forecast for one rooftop is beyond what our data can test. Cross-study accuracy comparisons are unreliable, because climate, horizon, aggregation and normalisation differ (Supplementary material), and we claim no accuracy advantage over published work.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fleet and season.} Thirty-three rooftops in one country and one NWP regime, with no east- or west-facing system. The main test window covers mid-February to May; rolling windows and calibration start dates in other seasons (Supplementary material) support the main conclusions but use shorter training histories.
\item \textbf{Reference.} SARAH-3 is a retrieval with its own uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:rw-reference}); no ground pyranometer was available.
\item \textbf{Metadata used as supplied.} The nine near-flat tilt entries were not filtered. Their median zero-shot $R^2$ (0.705) does not differ significantly from the rest of the fleet (0.741; Mann--Whitney $p = 0.56$). Coordinates were not perturbed, although \citet{Depoortere2025} show that location errors can remove the transfer advantage.
\item \textbf{Selection on test data.} The ensemble feature and the treatment of system 81376 were chosen after test results were seen; the gains they bring are therefore optimistic.
\item \textbf{Hyperparameter searches.} Both were budget-limited, and tree counts reached or approached their caps. They were run before the timing correction and were not repeated after it. The transfer search covered P50 only, so the P25 and P90 models in deployment use the temporal configuration.
\item \textbf{Probabilistic scope.} The quantiles were chosen for decisions rather than to describe the full distribution, so CRPS is not reported; the P10 model reuses the P25 hyperparameters.
\item \textbf{Learner and ensemble.} Only LightGBM was run under the final protocol (Section~\ref{sec:meth-model}). Lagged and multi-model constructions stand in for a true initial-condition ensemble, which we could not retrieve for the study window.

\end{itemize}
