\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The European Union reached a cumulative PV capacity of 406~GW at the end of 2025, after adding 65.1~GW in that year \citep{SolarPowerEurope2025}. Belgium is among the most densely penetrated markets: its capacity approached 13~GW in 2025, roughly 1,080~W per capita \citep{IEAPVPS2026}. Much of it sits on residential rooftops: a large population of small, sparsely documented generators whose orientation, tilt and even rated capacity are known only approximately.

Their output must be predicted a day in advance. \ifepsr Distribution system operators use day-ahead forecasts to anticipate reverse power flow and congestion on low-voltage feeders. Aggregators and balance-responsible parties bid distributed capacity into day-ahead markets and carry the imbalance cost of forecast error.\else Aggregators bid distributed capacity into day-ahead markets.\fi\ Home energy-management systems schedule overnight battery charging against expected generation. Each use needs generation at sub-hourly resolution for the following day, from a forecast issued around noon. At this horizon, numerical weather prediction (NWP) is in practice the only source of information about tomorrow's sky \citep{Inman2013,Antonanzas2016}.

PV forecasting methods have moved from persistence to tree ensembles, deep sequence models and transformers \citep{Antonanzas2016,Yang2018}. A meta-analysis of 320 studies \citep{Nguyen2026} found that, at the day-ahead horizon, NWP input is worth +11.6 percentage points of skill score, about 1.6 times the largest model-family effect (+7.0 points for ensemble-hybrid models). Reported skill also rose 10 points from 2021 to 2022 against 3 points from 2006 to 2019, which points to selection bias rather than a step change in capability. Such cross-study analysis cannot separate, on one dataset, the error the current input leaves unexplained from the value of better weather information. Bounding the latter needs an experiment that supplies information the operational forecast cannot have.

Three further problems are seldom quantified together on one dataset (Section~\ref{sec:related}). First, the \emph{reference problem}: without ground sensors, NWP irradiance is often validated against ERA5 \citep{Hersbach2020}, which is produced by the same centre and model as the forecast under test. Satellite retrievals such as SARAH-3 \citep{Pfeifroth2024} are independent. Second, the \emph{propagation problem}: model output statistics (MOS) correct NWP irradiance on the premise that better irradiance gives better power \citep{Verzijlbergh2015}, which holds for a physical model chain \citep{Mayer2021,Mayer2022} but is rarely tested for a learner trained on the same biased input. Third, the \emph{transfer problem}: most reported accuracy comes from chronological splits in which every system is also in the training set, which overstates what a newly connected rooftop receives \citep{Depoortere2025}; the causes of transfer failure are seldom examined.

This study addresses four research questions for a fleet of residential rooftops:
\begin{itemize}
\item[RQ1.] How much would better weather information be worth, at fleet and local scale, and where does the remaining error lie?
\item[RQ2.] Does widening the coarse (0.25$^\circ$) NWP input, through lagged re-runs, multi-model averaging or ensemble dispersion, improve accuracy?
\item[RQ3.] How much does the validation reference distort measured irradiance error, and does a correction learned against an independent reference improve power forecasts?
\item[RQ4.] What accuracy does a rooftop absent from training receive, what drives the failures, and how much local history is needed before site calibration helps?
\end{itemize}

Each effect has been reported separately; the contribution is to quantify them together on one fleet, from irradiance through to power:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Oracle experiments that bound the value of fleet-wide and local weather knowledge from below, and a spatial analysis that locates the remaining error in the weather input (RQ1).
\item Evidence that averaging across meteorological centres helps while averaging re-runs of one centre does not, and that ensemble dispersion adds no probabilistic skill to a quantile model (RQ2).
\item A measurement of reanalysis-reference distortion and an end-to-end test showing that a working irradiance correction does not propagate to power, on seen and unseen rooftops (RQ3).
\item A deployment benchmark for unseen rooftops: the full outcome distribution, a hyperparameter-selection protocol for transfer, the role of effective capacity and site features, and the local history needed before calibration (RQ4).
\end{enumerate}
